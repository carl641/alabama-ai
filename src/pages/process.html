Step by step

What happens at each stage

You can stop after any stage and keep what's been delivered. Each stage ends with something concrete you can review.

  1. 1. Discovery

    An opportunity call, then short interviews with the people who do the work. We map the workflow as it really runs: the steps, the tools, the handoffs and the exceptions. We also ask what data the workflow touches and who is allowed to see it.

    You get: a written summary of the workflow and a go / no-go recommendation. Larger or unclear situations become a formal AI assessment.

  2. 2. Baseline

    Before building anything, we record how the workflow performs today: volumes, time per task, response times, error or drop-off rates, whatever matters for this problem. Where numbers don't exist, we agree a simple way to sample them.

    You get: a baseline and the success measures the pilot will be judged against.

    Typical duration: {{todo: Typical baseline duration.}}

  3. 3. Pilot scope and build

    We agree one narrow pilot in writing: inputs, outputs, systems it connects to, where a person approves, what it must never do, and what it costs. Then we build it, in your accounts wherever practical, using test or sample data first.

    You get: a working pilot, a written scope, and a demo session with the people who will use it.

    Typical duration: {{todo: Typical pilot build duration.}}

  4. 4. Testing

    We test against realistic cases, including awkward ones: incomplete information, angry customers, off-topic questions, system outages, and attempts to get the system to do something it shouldn't. Your team reviews outputs and flags anything wrong. Failures get fixed or designed around, and the remaining limits get written down.

    You get: a test log, known limitations, and a sign-off decision.

    Typical duration: {{todo: Typical testing duration.}}

  5. 5. Rollout

    We switch the system on gradually where possible: a limited set of cases, a limited time window or a single team first, with close monitoring. Staff are trained on the parts they touch, including how to override or switch it off.

    You get: a live system, trained staff and a comparison against the baseline once there's enough data.

    Typical duration: {{todo: Typical rollout duration.}}

  6. 6. Documentation

    Every system comes with plain-language documentation: what it does, what it connects to, which accounts and credentials it uses, where prompts and settings live, how to pause it, and who to call when something looks wrong.

    You get: a runbook your team, or another provider, can follow.

  7. 7. Support

    AI systems need upkeep. Vendors change models and pricing, your business changes its services and hours, and edge cases appear. Ongoing support covers monitoring, fixes, prompt and knowledge updates, and periodic re-testing. It's optional, and scoped separately.

Questions about the process

Can we skip the baseline?

You can, but we don't recommend it. Without a "before" measurement, nobody can say whether the system is working, and decisions about expanding or stopping it become guesses. For small projects the baseline can be a simple sample of a week's work.

What if the pilot doesn't meet its success measures?

Then we look at why. Sometimes a design change fixes it; sometimes the workflow isn't a good fit for AI. Either way you keep the documentation and findings, and you decide whether to adjust, try something else or stop.
