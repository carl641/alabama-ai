Who this is for

Firms where the bottleneck is senior people's time

This page is for management and IT consultancies, marketing and creative agencies, architecture, engineering and design firms, staffing and recruiting firms, and similar businesses that sell expertise by the project, retainer or hour.

The common thread is that your most expensive people end up doing work that doesn't need their expertise: re-reading an RFP to find the due date, reformatting last year's proposal, or pulling numbers into a client report. Those tasks follow patterns, and patterns are where AI drafting and automation help.

Law and accounting firms share many of these workflows but carry professional-conduct and confidentiality obligations that need specific review. We treat them case by case rather than covering them here.

Signs AI could help

  • Inquiries wait days for a first response because the right person is busy
  • Proposals are rebuilt from old ones by copy and paste
  • Staff read long documents to fill in a standard form or checklist
  • Monthly client reports take a day to assemble from several tools
  • Knowledge lives in people's heads and old email threads
Workflows

Four workflows we build for professional firms

Each one produces a draft or an organized summary for a person to act on. None of them sends advice, fees or commitments to a client without review.

01

Client intake and screening

Today: inquiries land in a shared inbox. Someone reads each one, asks follow-up questions, checks whether it's a fit, and eventually books a call.

With AI: each inquiry is summarized into a standard intake record: who they are, what they need, timeline, budget signals and what's missing. It's checked against your fit criteria, logged in your CRM, and answered with a reply asking for the missing details or offering a call time.

Human review: a person approves outgoing replies until you're comfortable with them, and every decline or referral is a human decision.

02

Proposal and SOW drafting

Today: a senior person opens the last similar proposal and spends hours adapting it.

With AI: from the intake record and discovery-call notes, the system drafts the proposal using your template, approved boilerplate and past language for similar work: background, approach, deliverables, assumptions and exclusions.

Human review: scope, fees, staffing and timelines are set by the proposal owner. AI drafts the words around those decisions; it doesn't make them.

03

Document extraction and review prep

Today: staff read RFPs, contracts, specifications or client files to pull out dates, requirements and obligations into a checklist.

With AI: a custom AI agent reads the document and fills your checklist, citing the page or section for each item so a reviewer can verify it quickly. It also flags anything it couldn't find.

Human review: the extract is a starting point. A qualified person checks it against the source before anyone relies on it.

04

Recurring client reporting

Today: someone exports data from several tools each month, builds charts, and writes the same kind of commentary.

With AI: workflow automation gathers the data on schedule, builds the report in your format, and drafts the narrative: what changed, what stood out, what to watch.

Human review: the account lead edits the commentary and adds the recommendations. Numbers are pulled directly from the source systems, not generated by AI.

Worked example

Illustrative example: an engineering firm's RFP week

Illustrative example with a fictional firm. It shows how the workflows connect, not results from a client project.

A fictional 25-person civil engineering firm receives a 60-page request for proposals on Monday. Instead of a project manager spending the afternoon reading it, the document agent produces a two-page summary: submission deadline, required forms, evaluation criteria, insurance requirements and mandatory meeting dates, each with a page reference. Two items it couldn't find are flagged.

The principal decides to pursue it. From the summary and a 20-minute team discussion, the system drafts the technical approach, pulling relevant project descriptions and staff bios from the firm's approved library. The principal rewrites the approach section, sets the fee, and a coordinator handles final formatting.

The firm's judgment is all still there. What's gone is the reading, searching and reformatting around it.

What this depends on

  • A library of approved proposal language, project descriptions and bios
  • Clear fit criteria for new work
  • A named reviewer for each output type
  • Agreement on which documents may be processed by which AI services
  • Source citations on every extracted fact

Limits

Where AI shouldn't be trusted on its own

  • Giving professional advice or opinions to clients
  • Setting fees, scope or staffing
  • Stating facts about a document without a citation a person can check
  • Writing anything that goes out under a licensed professional's name without their review
  • Processing client data your engagement terms don't allow you to share with a third-party service

Questions from professional firms

Will AI-written proposals sound generic?

They will if they're generated from nothing. We build drafting around your own approved language and past proposals, so the draft sounds like your firm. A person still edits it, and those edits improve the library over time.

Is our client data used to train AI models?

Our approach is to choose services and account settings where your data isn't used for model training, and to document that choice for each tool. Confirm the specifics for your project on a call, and see our security approach.

Can AI read scanned PDFs and messy documents?

Usually, with lower reliability on poor scans, handwriting and complex tables. That's one reason every extracted fact comes with a page reference and anything uncertain is flagged for a person.

We're a law or accounting firm. Can you help?

Possibly, case by case. Those professions have specific confidentiality and conduct rules, and we'd want to understand them for your firm before proposing anything. We don't claim regulatory compliance credentials we don't hold.

Should we train our staff first or automate first?

Often both. Firms can get quick value from teaching staff to use AI assistants well and safely, alongside one automated workflow. See AI training.
