The scenario

Four calls worth trying

Every branch starts the same way: the assistant says it's an automated assistant, then asks how it can help. Each path shows a different rule in action.

Routine repair booking

The caller's air conditioner has stopped. The assistant first checks for anything dangerous, then collects name, callback number, a description of the problem and the service address, offers sample time slots and books one pending dispatcher review. It won't quote prices.

Gas smell: emergency

The assistant stops the intake immediately. It tells the caller to get out of the house, avoid anything that could make a spark, and call the gas utility's emergency line or 911, then pages the on-call technician and attempts a live transfer.

"Can I talk to a person?"

It doesn't argue or try to keep the caller. It explains the office is closed, offers a callback, and checks whether the problem is urgent enough to page the on-call technician tonight.

A question it can't answer

Asked whether a home warranty will cover a repair, it says it doesn't know rather than guessing, and offers to take a message for the office team.

Approval & handoff design

Who decides what

The most important part of an AI receptionist isn't the conversation. It's the set of rules that decide when the conversation stops being the assistant's job.

  • Safety first, always. Keywords and situations like gas, smoke, carbon monoxide or no heat in freezing weather skip straight to safety instructions and escalation. The assistant never tries to troubleshoot them.
  • A person can always be reached. Asking for a human is honored every time, as a transfer or a scheduled callback.
  • No commitments it can't keep. Bookings are held pending dispatcher review; prices, warranties and exceptions are passed to staff.
  • Disclosure up front. Callers are told they're speaking with an automated assistant.
  • Every call leaves a record. Your team gets a structured summary with a clear next action, whatever the outcome.

Limits

What this demo doesn't show

  • Real speech. Callers mumble, interrupt, change their minds and call from noisy trucks. A voice system has to handle that; a button click doesn't.
  • Real calendars. The time slots are hard-coded. A real build reads and writes to your scheduling system.
  • Real alerts. "Paging the on-call technician" is simulated. In production it's a text, call or app alert with a fallback if nobody answers.
  • Accuracy or performance. A script always follows the path you choose. Real systems need testing against realistic calls before and after launch.

Building a real one

What a production receptionist needs

  1. Your call rules

    What counts as an emergency, who's on call, service area, which jobs you book and which you don't.

  2. Phone and system access

    A phone number or forwarding setup, plus connections to your scheduling or CRM tool and your alerting method.

  3. Scripts and knowledge

    Approved answers to common questions, and a clear list of what the assistant must never answer.

  4. Testing and review

    Scenario testing before launch, then regular review of call summaries and handoffs. Recording, consent and any regulated uses should be reviewed with qualified advisers.
