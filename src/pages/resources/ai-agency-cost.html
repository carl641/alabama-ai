How Much Does an AI Agency Cost?

An honest answer depends on what you're buying: an assessment, a pilot, a production build, ongoing support, or the software and usage fees underneath all of them. This guide explains each cost, what moves it up or down, and how to compare proposals that are structured differently.

The direct answer

There is no single market price for "an AI agency." The cost of a project is driven mostly by how many systems it touches, how messy the underlying data and process are, how much human review it needs, and whether you're paying for a one-off build or an ongoing relationship. A narrow workflow that connects two tools you already use costs far less than an agent that reads documents, makes decisions and writes into several business systems.

We won't give you a market-rate range here, because we don't have a published, reliable source for one, and invented averages would do you more harm than good. Instead, this guide gives you the parts of the bill so you can ask for them by name and compare proposals line by line.

What drives the cost

When two providers quote very different numbers for what sounds like the same project, the difference usually traces back to one of these factors.

Scope: one workflow or several

"Automate our intake" can mean one web form feeding one spreadsheet, or every channel (phone, email, website, walk-ins) feeding a CRM, a scheduling tool and an accounting system. Each additional channel, system and exception adds design, build and testing time.

Integrations and access

Connecting to modern software with a documented API is usually straightforward. Older desktop software, systems with no API, or tools where your plan tier doesn't include API access can multiply the effort, or force a workaround such as scheduled exports.

Data and process readiness

AI works from the information you give it. If your service descriptions, pricing rules or policies live in people's heads, someone has to write them down before anything can be built. That work is real, and it's often underestimated by both sides.

Risk and human review

A system that drafts internal notes needs less safeguarding than one that talks to customers, quotes prices or touches financial records. Approval steps, fallbacks to a person, logging and testing against bad inputs all take time to build, and they're worth paying for.

Who maintains it afterward

A build you'll maintain yourself needs documentation and training. A build the agency maintains needs a support agreement. Either way, somebody's time is being paid for.

Engagement types, and what each one buys

Most AI agency work falls into a handful of engagement types. Proposals often combine several, so it helps to separate them.

EngagementWhat you getHow it's usually priced
AssessmentA baseline of how work happens today, a ranked list of opportunities and a scoped first project.Fixed fee, sized by the number of workflows and interviews.
PilotOne workflow built and tested with real users on a limited scale, with success measures agreed up front.Fixed fee or capped time-and-materials.
Production buildA system hardened for everyday use: error handling, monitoring, documentation, handover.Fixed fee per defined scope, or milestones.
Support retainerMonitoring, fixes, prompt and rule updates, small changes, and someone to call.Monthly fee with a defined allowance of hours or requests.
Software and usage feesAI model usage, phone minutes, automation platform plans, hosting.Paid monthly, often metered; sometimes passed through by the agency, sometimes billed to you directly.
TrainingSessions that teach staff to use AI tools well and safely.Per session or per cohort.

The line that surprises people most is the last row of recurring fees. A build is a one-time cost; model usage, phone minutes and platform subscriptions continue every month the system runs, and some scale with volume. Ask for an estimate at your expected volume and at double it.

Worked example: reading a proposal

Illustrative example. The business and every dollar figure below are hypothetical, chosen to make the arithmetic easy to follow. They are not quotes, not our prices and not market averages.

A 12-person heating and cooling company misses calls after hours and follows up on web leads slowly. It receives a proposal with these lines:

  • Assessment: $3,000 (hypothetical)
  • Build of an after-hours AI receptionist plus automatic lead follow-up: $12,000 (hypothetical)
  • Support retainer: $800 a month (hypothetical)
  • Estimated usage and phone fees: $150 a month at current call volume (hypothetical)

The first-year cost is not $15,000. It's $3,000 + $12,000 + 12 × ($800 + $150) = $26,400, plus the owner's and office manager's time for interviews, testing and training, which the proposal doesn't mention.

Next, the company checks that number against its own baseline rather than the vendor's promise. It counts after-hours calls that went to voicemail over a typical month, how many of those callers never booked, and what an average job is worth after costs. If the realistic recovered work covers the first-year cost with room to spare, the project is worth a pilot. If it only works on optimistic assumptions, the company should narrow the scope, perhaps starting with lead follow-up alone, or wait.

Two cautions from this example apply to any proposal. First, staff hours saved are not cash saved unless you actually redeploy or reduce paid time. Second, ask what happens to the monthly fees if you stop the retainer: can the system keep running, and who owns it?

How to compare quotes that are structured differently

One provider quotes a fixed build fee and a monthly retainer. Another quotes hourly with no retainer. A third offers a monthly subscription that bundles everything. Headline numbers can't be compared directly, so put every proposal on the same basis.

  1. List every cost line under three headings: one-time, recurring fixed, and recurring metered (usage that grows with volume).
  2. Price metered lines at your real volume. Use your own baseline, such as calls per month, leads per month or documents per week, and ask each provider to estimate usage fees at that number.
  3. Add them up over one year and over three years. A low build fee with a high monthly subscription can overtake a higher build fee within the first year or two.
  4. Add your internal time at a rough hourly value. A provider that needs twice as much of your office manager's time is more expensive than its invoice suggests.
  5. Note what you'd own at the end. A subscription you can't take with you and a build handed over with documentation are not the same purchase, even at the same price.
  6. Check the scope matches. If one quote includes testing, documentation and training and another doesn't, you're comparing different projects.

When the totals are close, choose the proposal with clearer scope, clearer failure handling and better ownership terms. Those are the parts that get expensive later.

Costs proposals often leave out

  • Your team's time. Interviews, reviewing drafts, testing, and learning the new process. Budget it like any other cost.
  • Software plan upgrades. Some integrations need a higher tier of software you already use, for example to unlock API access.
  • Knowledge preparation. Writing down policies, prices and answers the system needs. Someone has to own this.
  • Usage growth. Metered fees rise with volume. A busy season can cost more than the month the estimate was based on.
  • Change requests. Adding a new service, location or form after launch. Ask how small changes are priced.
  • Model and vendor changes. AI providers update and retire models. Someone has to retest the system when that happens.
  • Exit costs. Moving the system to another provider or in-house if the relationship ends.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. What exactly is in scope, and what is explicitly out of scope?
  2. Which recurring fees will we pay, to whom, and what's your estimate at our volume and at twice our volume?
  3. Are software and usage fees billed to us directly or passed through with a markup?
  4. What does the support fee cover, and what counts as a billable change?
  5. Who owns the workflows, prompts, code and accounts when the project ends?
  6. What measure will tell us the pilot worked, and what happens if it doesn't?
  7. How much of our staff time will the project need, and when?
  8. What happens when the AI is unsure or wrong? Who gets notified, and how fast?

A provider who answers these plainly, in writing, is easier to budget for than one with a lower headline number and vague answers. Our guide to choosing an AI agency covers the rest of the evaluation.

When not to buy

  • You can't describe the workflow yet. Pay for an assessment or map the process yourself first. Building on a vague process just automates the confusion.
  • The volume is tiny. If a task happens a few times a month, the setup and monthly fees may never pay back.
  • Simple software would do it. A booking link, a form or a scheduled report can beat an AI build on cost and reliability. See AI vs. traditional automation.
  • The proposal can't name its recurring costs. If nobody can estimate the monthly bill, you can't budget for it.
  • Nobody on your side will own it. Every system needs an internal owner who notices when something looks wrong.
